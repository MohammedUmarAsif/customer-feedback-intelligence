First, the human problem

If people keep asking for help, what should change?

Imagine a support team receiving messages about confusing bills, account access, and requests that go unresolved. Answering each person matters. So does noticing when many people face the same problem.

Two different questions: What should the team investigate? And can new messages be sent to the right product team? Counting helps with the first. A text-classification model can help with the second.

  1. Clean the feedbackRemove repeated entries so they do not inflate patterns.
  2. Find recurring issuesCount problems and trace each finding back to its evidence.
  3. Test a routing modelCompare against a simple baseline on later, unseen complaints.
  4. Keep people involvedFlag uncertain suggestions instead of treating every prediction as reliable.
The real data behind this story

This demo uses complaints people submitted about bank accounts, cards, loans, and other financial products in the United States. The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau (CFPB), a US government agency, publishes this complaint data. A “narrative” simply means the person's written account.

We selected 12,000 written complaints from January–June 2026 and removed 1,050 repeated or very similar entries. The remaining 10,950 form this study. These are historical public complaint records.

3. Can a model help sort the next message?

“Routing” means choosing a product category, such as Mortgage or Credit card. The model reads a complaint and suggests a category. When its score is low, it asks for human review. It does not resolve the complaint.

4. What would make this useful in a real service?

The prototype delivers a repeatable way to find patterns and test message sorting. Proving that it improves a service needs one more step: a real-world pilot.

For a customer-support team

Use the recurring issues to choose a focused case review. Check the actual messages and service records before changing a process.

For a product team

Turn the review into a testable change: clearer instructions, fewer handoffs, or better status updates. Measure whether customers need to contact support again.

A testable next step: try clearer instructions with one eligible group and compare it with the current process. Measure repeat-contact rates and check for worse outcomes. This pilot has not been run.

Research and ideas behind the approach

Scikit-learn's text-classification example explains turning words into features and comparing classifiers. This project uses TF-IDF and logistic regression as a compact baseline.

Wang, Zhu and Chen (June 2026 preprint) studies complaint text using earlier data for development and later data for testing. It predicts monetary relief; this project predicts a product category. Its model and scores are not this project's results.

A newer or larger model would need to beat this baseline on fresh data and justify its cost. No large language model is used for the classifier in this demo.

What remains unproven

Complaint counts show what appears in this sample. Without knowing the total number of customers or transactions, we cannot calculate a customer problem rate. The findings do not prove causes, customer satisfaction, cost savings, or business impact.

Each month was sampled separately. Six months cannot establish seasonal patterns, and duplicate detection can miss reworded complaints. Model performance varies by category; some rare categories perform poorly.
